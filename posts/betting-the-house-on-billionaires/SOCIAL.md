The first two lines are what people see before "see more", so they carry the number.

```
Watch how much Walmart and the Walton family have meant to their hometown, Benton County, Arkansas.

Its labor force is up 71% over the past 20 years. Neighboring Washington County is up 41%. The rest of Arkansas, the other 73 counties, is down 1.3%.

Put another way: Benton and Washington added 115,303 workers over those 20 years. The other 73 counties lost 14,576 between them. Those two counties are the only reason Arkansas has more people working or looking for work today than it did in 2006.

Forty-five of those other 73 counties are in what we call structural loss, meaning a labor force more than 10% below where it stood 20 years earlier.

We found the same shape in Nebraska. The state's fastest-growing county is Sarpy, next door to Warren Buffett's Omaha, up 52%. Fifty-nine of Nebraska's 93 counties are in structural loss. In January 2010 it was four.

None of this proves causality, and we say so plainly in the post. Benton County has more going on than one company: more than 1,600 Walmart supplier offices sit within a short drive, and the Walton family spent decades and a great deal of money turning Bentonville into a mountain biking destination. Saline County, outside Little Rock, is growing just as fast with no Walton connection at all.

But it is hard not to wonder what would have happened to these places if those fortunes had been built somewhere else. That question, and what it says about how much of America's growth now rests on very few people, is today's Data 4 Thought.
```
