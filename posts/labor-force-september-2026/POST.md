# September 2026 update: 35% of counties in structural labor force loss

## The national picture

The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) posted county labor force data through September 2026 on October 28, 2026. In September, 1,132 of 3,214 U.S. counties and Puerto Rico municipios were in structural labor force loss. That means their labor force was more than 10% below the same month 20 years earlier. It works out to 35.2% of all counties. The map below, from our free [interactive labor force map](https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/labor-force-history-viz), shows every county's 20-year change, month by month, through September 2026.

Looking at the 3,207 counties with data in both years, the count rose by 40 from September 2025. In all, 129 counties entered the structural loss group while 89 left it.

The chart below shows the share of counties in structural loss for every September since 2012. September 2026 is the third highest, behind two post-COVID years: 2022 (36.2%) and 2021 (35.5%).

## The other five bands

Structural loss is one of six bands. Fast growth has become rarer. In September 2026, 374 counties (11.6%) had a labor force at least 20% larger than 20 years earlier, the Superstars and Hyper-Growth bands together. That is the lowest share of any September since 2012, when it was 983 counties (30.6%).

At the other end, 60.3% of counties had a smaller labor force than 20 years earlier, counting structural loss and at-risk contraction together. That is close to 2021 and 2022 (61.0% in both) and up from 30.4% in 2012.

## But can we trust the latest data?

One state accounts for half of the net increase of 40 counties. In Michigan, 20 counties entered structural loss in the past year and none left. The next largest net gains were in Minnesota (10), Oklahoma (9) and Tennessee (9).

For now, we are treating these numbers with caution. County labor force figures are built from state estimates that lean heavily on a household survey, and in 2026 that survey has drifted away from the counts of jobs that employers report. In September, the one-year change in employed residents trailed the change in jobs by 2 points or more in 14 states, and Michigan's gap was the largest. From 2015 to 2019, that never happened in more than four states in a month.

The chart below shows each month since 2015. For each state, we compare two numbers: how much the count of employed residents (based on the household survey) changed from a year earlier, and how much the count of jobs (as reported by employers) changed. If those two numbers are within 2 points of each other, the state is not counted. Michigan in September is a state that is counted. Its employed residents fell 5.0% while its jobs were about flat (up 0.03%), a gap of about 5 points.

Each pair of bars is one month. A bar below the line counts the states where employed residents trailed jobs by 2 points or more, like Michigan. A bar above the line counts the states where employed residents ran ahead of jobs by 2 points or more. When the two measures roughly agree, as they did in most months from 2015 to 2019, the bars stay short. A tall bar means many states got very different answers from the two measures in the same month.

In 2020, COVID was a shock to our labor force and to the surveys that measure it. Models that hadn't really been stressed in the past were stressed, and BLS tried to make changes in real time. It reminds me of a shock to a refinery and the control room operator trying to monkey with the valves to get the system back to homeostasis. But if you move the valves too much, you risk making the problem worse. Something like that may be happening now. The result is that the two primary measures of our workforce are spitting out conflicting signals for some states. One says Michigan is losing employed residents at an alarming clip. The count of jobs says nothing is changing. Not knowing which one is correct may be even scarier than knowing.

We will be coming back to this wonky but immensely important topic in the coming weeks and months. Our goal here at Data 4 The People is to bring public data to you in a form that can bring insight, spur discussion, and lead to positive change. But we have to go into working with public data with a skeptical eye, especially as government agencies rely on models stretched thin to gross up surveys with [worsening response rates](https://www.bls.gov/cps/methods/response_rates.htm). We'll do our best to uncover discrepancies between datasets to help you understand what to trust, and what to hold lightly.

## What this does not tell you

- County labor force figures are model estimates, not direct counts. BLS controls them to a state or area total, so a small county's number is partly a share of a larger estimate.
- The 2026 figures are preliminary and are expected to change in the early-2027 revision.
- Employed residents and job counts measure different things: people where they live versus jobs where they are. They are not expected to match exactly. Job counts from employer tax records (QCEW) are about six months behind, so April to September 2026 uses the payroll survey, which is itself a sample survey and will be checked against QCEW when those counts come out.
- Seven New Orleans-area parishes have no 20-year comparison for September 2025 because their September 2005 data are missing after Hurricane Katrina. That is why the year-over-year comparison uses 3,207 counties.
- We do not yet know what is causing the gap between the surveys and the job counts. The refinery comparison is a possibility, not a finding.

## How we built this

Each county is compared with its own labor force in the same month 20 years earlier, using BLS Local Area Unemployment Statistics (not seasonally adjusted). A county is in structural loss when that change is below -10.0%. Comparisons over time always use the same month. For the survey check, we compare each state's one-year change in employed residents (Local Area Unemployment Statistics) with its one-year change in jobs, month by month, not seasonally adjusted: jobs from the BLS Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages through March 2026, and from the BLS Current Employment Statistics payroll survey after that. Code and method notes are in our [GitHub repository](https://github.com/Data4ThePeople/laus). For a guide to reading the map, see [Five takeaways from our new labor force map](https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/five-takeaways-labor-force-decline).
